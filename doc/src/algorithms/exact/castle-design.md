This page gathers the design of castle arithmetic ([](gr_ca),
[](gr_tower)) as a whole: the ideas it rests on, the architecture of the
implementation, the comparison with the legacy design of [](ca), the
lessons of its development and the missing pieces. The algorithms
themselves are described on the other pages of this chapter. The
companion document [@Joh2026tower] describes the same material at
greater length; it predates the renaming of the user-facing layer (the
*lazy tower fields* of `gr_tower_lazy.h`, now the castle fields of
`gr_ca.h`) and the special functions of
[](algorithms/exact/castle-hypergeometric) and
[](algorithms/exact/castle-modular).

## Three ideas and one principle {#ideas}

Castle arithmetic was designed to make the zero test complete where
theory allows (unconditionally for algebraic numbers, conditionally on
Schanuel's conjecture for the elementary numbers and on similar
conjectures for some special function values), to keep every answer
proved, and to be at least as fast as Calcium on the workloads where
Calcium already succeeds. The design rests on three ideas, none of them
new in isolation.

1. *Towers instead of unordered fields.* Generators are adjoined in a
   definition order, each algebraic generator $a_k$ with a monic modulus
   $m_k$ over the field $F_{k-1}$ generated by the earlier ones. The
   moduli form a triangular set, which is automatically a lexicographic
   Gröbner basis; no Buchberger completion is ever needed
   ([](algorithms/exact/field-towers#prop-groebner)).
2. *Dynamic evaluation* (the D5 principle). A modulus need not be known
   to be irreducible. Each quotient ring pretends to be a field; when an
   inversion meets a zero divisor, the modulus is replaced by the factor
   that vanishes at the generator's numerical value, and existing
   elements remain valid. This makes the zero test for algebraic numbers
   complete without computing minimal polynomials
   ([](algorithms/exact/dynamic-evaluation)).
3. *Richardson's structure-theorem approach* for transcendental
   generators. When an element reduces to a nonzero rational function of
   transcendental generators, an integer relation among the logarithmic
   quantities of the tower is sought numerically and verified exactly;
   if one exists, the top generator involved becomes algebraic over the
   earlier ones (of degree one or as a radical), in place. Under
   Schanuel's conjecture this search always succeeds when the element is
   zero; when no relation exists, numerical evaluation eventually proves
   the element nonzero ([](algorithms/exact/zero-testing#castle)).

Around these ideas the implementation adds irreducibility certificates
(modular places, Capelli's theorem, Eisenstein criteria, Trager norms),
factorization and root finding over towers, structured generators for
roots of unity, radicals and Gauss sums, real fields with real
generators and a real form of Richardson's algorithm, relations among
values of special functions, and a layer in which elements of the four
exact fields carry pointers to shared towers that are created, merged,
grown and collected on demand.

The guiding principle is: *every answer is proved; conjectures affect
only completeness.* The implementation never returns `T_TRUE` or
`T_FALSE` on the strength of a conjecture or a heuristic. Schanuel's
conjecture, the Rohrlich–Lang conjecture on $\Gamma$ values, the
Chowla–Milnor conjecture on Hurwitz zeta values and similar statements
are used only to predict that a search will succeed; when they are false
(or when a resource limit is reached) the outcome is `T_UNKNOWN`
(`GR_UNABLE` in the `gr` interface), never a wrong answer.

The base field of all towers is $\mathbb{Q}$. The code names, through
capability bits (`GR_TOWER_CAP_RATIONAL`, `GR_TOWER_CAP_FACTOR`,
`GR_TOWER_CAP_PLACES`, `GR_TOWER_CAP_EMBEDDING`,
`GR_TOWER_CAP_INVOLUTION`, `GR_TOWER_CAP_ORDERED`), every place where
another constant field (a number field, a finite field, the $p$-adic
numbers) would require work, but none is implemented.

### Related work {#related}

The classical approaches to exact real and complex arithmetic are
expression-DAG systems with numerical evaluation and separation bounds
[@vdH2006], which can prove nonzeroness and, for algebraic numbers, zero
via root separation bounds, and computer algebra systems that work with
algebraic numbers through minimal polynomials [@Str1997] or through
dynamic evaluation [@DDDD1985; @Duv1994; @Ste2010]. Steel's approach to
computing in algebraically closed fields [@Ste2002; @Ste2010] is close in
spirit to the algebraic part of castle arithmetic: fields are towers of
extensions by polynomials not known to be irreducible, and factors are
discovered when needed. The theory of triangular sets and regular chains
[@Laz1991; @Kal1993; @ALM1999] provides the algebraic framework for towers
given by triangular sets; fast arithmetic modulo triangular sets
[@LMS2009] and the complexity of the D5 principle [@DMSX2006] have been
studied in that setting. For transcendental constants, Richardson's work
[@Ric1992; @RF1994; @Ric1997; @Ric2007] and the structure theorems of
Risch [@Ris1979] and of Rothstein and Caviness [@RC1979] are the
foundation; the simplification of radicals [@Zip1985; @JR1999] and of
large symbolic expressions [@BBK2014] are related problems.

## Layers {#layers}

The implementation is organized in four layers.

1. *Generic primitives* in existing FLINT modules: the quotient rings of
   [](gr_domains#poly-quotient) with the pretend-field interface
   ([](algorithms/exact/dynamic-evaluation#pretend)), and the new
   primitives listed below.
2. *Towers* ([](gr_tower)): generator records, nested and flat
   representations, adjunction, maps, refinement, irreducibility proofs,
   the zero test and the relation searches, factorization, dense
   kernels. Elements of the fields $F_k$ are available through `gr`
   contexts (`gr_ctx_init_tower_field` and its flat variant), which
   record their level and remain valid when steps are appended; after a
   rebuild (a transcendental generator adjoined) they become stale and
   return `GR_UNABLE` rather than misinterpret elements.
3. *Castle fields* ([](gr_ca)): contexts, the element union (a public,
   read-only type with locked accessors), locking (each operation has an
   unlocked implementation called with the lock held and a thread-safe
   entry point, which is the method of the `gr` table, with lock-free
   paths for rational and dense elements), registries, merging, generator
   policies, elementary and special functions, real views, printing and
   parsing, effort limits.
4. *Interfaces*: the `gr` method tables (so that generic polynomial,
   matrix and series code runs over the fields), the Python classes of
   `flint_ctypes.py` (`ComplexField_castle`, `RealField_castle`,
   `ComplexAlgebraicField_castle`, `RealAlgebraicField_castle`, with
   tuning options as keyword arguments), and conversions to and from
   [](acb), [](arb) and [](qqbar).

*Example (one number in two layers).* In a fixed tower
$\mathbb{Q}(\pi)(a_1)$, $a_1 = \sqrt 2$, built with `gr_tower_adjoin_pi`
and `gr_tower_adjoin_sqrt`, the field context of the top level computes
$1/(\pi + \sqrt 2)$ as the nested element $\pi/(\pi^2 - 2) - a_1/(\pi^2 -
2)$, a polynomial in $a_1$ over $\mathbb{Q}(\pi)$. A castle field computes
the same number in a tower with the same two generators, and prints it in
flat form with the definitions: `(-a1+pi)/(pi^2-2) {a1 = sqrt(2)}`. A field
context of a tower outlives algebraic steps but not transcendental ones:
with $K = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt 2)$ as a context, adjoining $\sqrt 3$ leaves $K$
usable, while adjoining $\pi$ afterwards rebuilds the tower over
$\mathbb{Q}(\pi)$, and operations in $K$ then return `GR_UNABLE`
([](gr_tower#tower-field-context)).

### Statuses, failures and the gr contract {#statuses}

The implementation distinguishes the three outcomes of the `gr`
interface carefully. `GR_DOMAIN` means that the result does not exist
(division by zero, $\log 0$, a nonreal result in a real field, a
transcendental result in an algebraic field); it is returned only when
this is proved. `GR_UNABLE` means that the implementation could not
decide or compute the result within its limits; it is returned, in
particular, when a zero test ends as `T_UNKNOWN`, and inside the dynamic
evaluation machinery when a pretend field meets a zero divisor.
Mistaking one for the other was a recurring source of bugs (an inverse
returning `GR_DOMAIN` on a zero divisor would make a generic algorithm
conclude that a nonzero element is zero), and the pretend-field
interface was introduced in part to make the distinction explicit in
generic code.

*Example (domain errors and unable results).* In the castle fields, $1/0$
and $\log 0$ in $\mathbb{C}$, $\sqrt{-1}$ in $\mathbb{R}$ and $e^1$ in
$\overline{\mathbb{Q}}$ return `GR_DOMAIN` (in Python,
`FlintDomainError`). So does $1/x$ for $x = \sqrt 2 + \sqrt 3 - \sqrt{5 +
2\sqrt 6}$, since $x$ is proved to be 0. For $z = e^{\pi\sqrt{163}} -
640320^3 - 744 \approx -7.5 \cdot 10^{-13}$, a quantity about $10^{-30}$
times the size of its terms, $1/z$ succeeds with the default limits; in a
field whose three precision limits (`GR_TOWER_OPT_PREC_LIMIT`,
`GR_TOWER_OPT_CERTIFY_PREC_LIMIT`, `GR_TOWER_OPT_NUMERIC_PREC_LIMIT`) are
lowered to 64 bits, the zero test of $z$ ends as `T_UNKNOWN` and $1/z$
returns `GR_UNABLE` (`FlintUnableError`), which tells a generic algorithm
only that this element could not be used, not that it is zero. In the
unlimited mode the limits grow instead
([](algorithms/exact/effort-limits)).

### New FLINT primitives {#primitives}

The work produced or improved several general-purpose functions, each
with documentation and tests: `gr_poly_div_root` (synthetic division);
`gr_poly_quotient_norm` and `gr_poly_quotient_norm_poly` (norms down to
the base ring, defined as $\operatorname{Res}(m, A)$ so that they are
multiplicative); `acb_lindep` (all integer relations of the reduced
basis); `qqbar_set_fmpz_poly_root` (a root isolated by an enclosure,
certified); `fexpr_set_arb_decimal` and `fexpr_set_acb_decimal`;
`fmpz_mpoly_univar_pseudo_rem`; `n_randprime_mod` (random primes in a
progression); `fmpz_mpoly_q_get_fmpq`; the factored-denominator variant
of [](fmpz_mpoly_q); and the pretend-field interface of [](gr).
`fmpz_mpoly_compose_fmpz_mpoly_gen` was rewritten to be linear in the
number of variables, with a fast path for injective increasing renamings.
Two upstream bugs were found and fixed along the way: `qqbar_pow_fmpq`
returned a non-principal branch for roots of unity ($(-i)^{1/2}$ came out
as $-e^{-i\pi/4}$), and the functions that set generator names of
multivariate polynomial contexts freed the old names and then reallocated
the freed pointers.

*Example (relations and certified roots).* For the vector $(\log 2, \log
3, \log 6, \log 12)$ at 64 bits, `acb_lindep` returns two rows, $(-1, -1,
1, 0)$ and $(-1, 0, -1, 1)$, that is, $\log 6 = \log 2 + \log 3$ and $\log
12 = \log 2 + \log 6$: a basis of the relation lattice, where
`_qqbar_acb_lindep` returns only its first row. For $(\log 2, \log 3, \pi
i, \log(-6))$ it returns the single relation $\log(-6) = \log 2 + \log 3 +
\pi i$. The function `qqbar_set_fmpz_poly_root` turns $x^3 - x - 1$ and the
enclosure $[1.32 \pm 0.01]$ into the real root $1.32471\ldots$, and
$-0.66 + 0.56i$ (with radius 0.01 in each part) into a nonreal root, while
it returns 0 for the enclosure $[-0.66 \pm 0.01] + [0 \pm 0.6]i$, which
contains both nonreal roots. Over $\mathbb{Q}(t)$, the modulus $tx^2 - 1$
gives $x^2 = 1/t$; for $P = x^3 + 1$, `fmpz_mpoly_univar_pseudo_rem` with
respect to $x$ computes $t^2P = tx(tx^2 - 1) + tx + t^2$, so $P \equiv (tx
+ t^2)/t^2 = x/t + 1$ ([](algorithms/exact/field-towers#representations)).
Synthetic division (`gr_poly_div_root`) over $\mathbb{Q}(a)$, $a =
\sqrt[3]{2}$, divides $x^3 - 2$ by $x - a$ with quotient $x^2 + ax + a^2$
and remainder 0; this is the Cauchy modulus of the next conjugate of $a$
([](algorithms/exact/castle-roots#splitting)).

## What was inherited from Calcium, and what changed {#calcium}

The numerical and arithmetic substrate is inherited: Arb enclosures,
[](fmpz_mpoly_q) rational functions, [](qqbar), number field arithmetic in
the style of Antic, LLL with exact verification, truth values, the four
fields and their `gr` interface, printing with definitions and [](fexpr)
output. The algebraic organization changed: ordered towers with
triangular sets instead of unordered fields with Gröbner ideals, dynamic
evaluation instead of minimal polynomials, eliminations in place instead
of relations fixed at field creation, and a zero test that is complete
where theory allows instead of one that may return `T_UNKNOWN` on simple
identities.

| Aspect | Calcium ([](ca)) | Castle arithmetic ([](gr_ca)) |
|---|---|---|
| *Inherited (kept, possibly extended)* | | |
| Model | numbers as elements of explicit fields over $\mathbb{Q}$ with symbolic generators, exact arithmetic plus enclosures | same |
| Fields | $\mathbb{C}$, $\mathbb{R}$, $\overline{\mathbb{Q}}$, $\overline{\mathbb{Q}} \cap \mathbb{R}$ as `gr` contexts | same, as views of one shared state |
| Numerics | Arb enclosures with adaptive precision | same; precision escalation after the exact stages |
| Multivariate elements | `fmpz_mpoly_q` modulo an ideal | `fmpz_mpoly_q` modulo a triangular set (the flat form) |
| Algebraic numbers | [](qqbar) (minimal polynomial and isolating ball) | `qqbar` for conversions, guesses and minimal polynomials; not required for generators |
| Single-generator fields | Antic `nf_elem` | dense form on `fmpq_poly`, any degree, used by density |
| Same-field products | `ca_mat_mul_same_nf` | one packed product, also for multi-generator fields |
| Relations among logarithms and exponents | LLL, exact verification | same, extended (all reduced rows, Hermite normal form, branch checks, $W$, angles) |
| Integer factoring in radicals and logarithms | trial division to `CA_OPT_SMOOTH_LIMIT` | same bound, plus complete factoring of word-size cofactors and Gaussian primes |
| Hash-consing | fields cached in the context | towers in a registry, constants hash-consed, definition ids |
| Output | definitions of extension numbers, `fexpr` | same, plus round trip through strings and structural transfer |
| Options | verbosity, printing, precision limit, smoothness limit, trigonometric form, ... | these kept as tower options with names and ranges, and many more |
| *Changed* | | |
| Structure | unordered fields $\mathbb{Q}(a_1, \ldots, a_n)/I$ | ordered towers, triangular sets |
| Relations | reduction ideal computed at field creation, Gröbner completion under limits | relations discovered on demand and applied in place; no completion needed |
| Algebraic generators | minimal polynomial required (`qqbar`) | any squarefree modulus with an isolating enclosure; dynamic refinement |
| Zero test | sound, incomplete (`T_UNKNOWN`) | sound; complete for algebraic numbers, complete under Schanuel for elementary numbers |
| Transcendental relations | relations added to the ideal | top generator becomes algebraic (eliminations in place, primitive exponentials) |
| Irreducibility | not needed (minimal polynomials) | modular places, Capelli, Eisenstein, Mordell, unramified rules, Trager |
| Polynomials | roots via `qqbar` for algebraic coefficients | gcd by subresultants, Trager factorization over towers, roots over any tower |
| Roots of unity | $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_N)$ as one number field | prime-power decomposition (single field optional) |
| Square roots | $\sqrt A$, $A$ squarefree of either sign, one generator | products of prime radicals; $\sqrt{-A}$ one generator; Gauss sums with roots of unity |
| Real fields | restriction flag, complex representation | views with real generators ($\tan$, $\operatorname{atan}$, $\tan(\pi/M)$) and a real relation search |
| Special functions | special values | canonical arguments, normal forms, relation rounds for $\Gamma$, $\psi^{(m)}$, $\zeta(s, a)$, $\operatorname{Li}_s$, $K$, $E$, $W$; hypergeometric and modular functions |
| Fractions | numerator and denominator reduced separately, gcd cancelled; not canonical | algebraic denominators cleared by norms: canonical, denominators in the transcendental generators; fractions kept beyond a size limit |
| Linear algebra | elimination with divisions | measured division-free policies; modular inverses |
| Field management | fields created per combination of extension numbers | lazy merging, in-place growth, prefix and subset towers, reference-counted collection |
| Concurrency | not thread-safe | per-context recursive lock; lock-free rational and dense paths |

The most important changes are the first four rows of the second part.
They are what make the zero test complete: a triangular set needs no
completion, dynamic evaluation needs no minimal polynomials, and
in-place eliminations let relations be applied whenever they are
discovered, so that a field never contains a relation it cannot use. Most
of the other changes are consequences or optimizations. One of them has a
large practical effect: because algebraic denominators are cleared by
norms ([](algorithms/exact/castle-representations#canonical)), flat
representatives in proven towers are canonical fractions, so that
equality, hashing and the cancellation in long sums are syntactic, where
Calcium's multivariate fractions need a cross multiplication and a
reduction modulo the ideal to be compared.

## Findings from the development process {#findings}

The implementation was developed over some fifty iterations, each ending
with a measured and tested state. The wrong answers come first, since
they are the most instructive: each was a violation of a hypothesis that
a proof relied upon, and each was found by a test designed to make
computations meet in unusual orders.

*Wrong answers and what they taught.*

1. *Proofs are relative to the field below.* The identity $\sqrt 2(1 +
   i)/2 = e^{\pi i/4}$ was refuted: the modulus $x^2 - 2$ had been proved
   irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}(i)$, later $\zeta_8$ was moved in front of
   $\sqrt 2$, the status stayed proven, but $x^2 - 2$ splits over
   $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_8)$, and the shortcut "all steps proven, so the
   representative is canonical" declared a true identity false. Every
   move now downgrades the generators it moves behind, unless a
   structural rule covers the new prefix
   ([](algorithms/exact/dynamic-evaluation#reordering)).
2. *A place of a product of fields sees one factor.* Gosper's identity
   was decided false after certain other computations in the same field:
   a square root above a dynamic step was proved irreducible at a place
   that saw only one factor of the step, and kept that status after the
   step was refined to the other factor. Modular proofs and Capelli tests
   at places now require the steps below to be proven
   ([](algorithms/exact/dynamic-evaluation#places)). Found by running a
   catalog of problems in one shared field in random orders.
3. *Upstream code can be wrong too.* Randomized comparison with [](qqbar)
   found that `qqbar_pow_fmpq` chose a non-principal branch for roots of
   unity: the disagreement was in the oracle. Oracles are only as good as
   their independence: `qqbar` and ball arithmetic share no code with the
   towers, which is why disagreements with them are informative in both
   directions. Generic quotient rings, likewise, exposed bugs in generic
   code paths (a scalar division of zero that skipped the division-by-zero
   check; generic divisibility predicates that returned `T_UNKNOWN` for
   pretend fields where the field answer is known).

*Algebraic lessons.*

4. *One number field, one generator.* Algebraic numbers of one number
   field must enter the tower through one generator; otherwise the
   compositum is never discovered except by expensive dynamic evaluation
   ([](algorithms/exact/field-towers#radicals)). This shaped the treatment
   of sines in $\Gamma$ formulas, the tangent normal form, the prime-power
   decomposition of radicals in eliminations, and Gauss sums for square
   roots in cyclotomic towers.
5. *Canonical arguments must be intrinsic*, a function of the value, not
   of the representation
   ([](algorithms/exact/castle-special-functions#principles)).
6. *Tolerate dependent generators; eliminate them later.* Refusing to
   create a $\Gamma(p/q)$ generator when the normal form failed was the
   wrong fix; the right one was a relation search for such generators
   ([](algorithms/exact/castle-special-functions#gamma-rational)). The
   same pattern (allow, then discover) is dynamic evaluation applied to
   every kind of generator.
7. *A relation is only useful in a good form.* Eliminations must avoid
   algebraic denominators, and relations should be integral combinations
   of the original ones (Hermite normal form rather than row reduction
   over $\mathbb{Q}$), so that radicals get integer exponents.
8. *Real forms do not mix with complex forms*
   ([](algorithms/exact/castle-real-fields#rem-forms)).

*Engineering lessons.*

9. *Representations are an empirical question.* Whether nested or flat
   arithmetic should carry the computations was settled by measurement
   ([](algorithms/exact/field-towers#representations)); arguments about
   asymptotic complexity pointed the other way. The thresholds of the
   linear algebra policies, the crossovers of reduction methods and the
   choice between Euclidean and subresultant gcds were all decided by
   benchmarks, and several were reversed by later measurements.
10. *Changing form costs more than either form.* When the dense form was
    extended to any degree, a running sum in $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{9973})$
    became 2.6 times slower because of conversions, not because of either
    form; the fix was a rule on operations rather than on elements
    ([](algorithms/exact/castle-representations#density)).
11. *History must not slow down new computations.* Searches over
    everything made later computations slower than the same computation
    in a fresh context, sometimes by factors of several hundred. Prefix
    copies, subset towers and the restriction to involved generators were
    the answers ([](algorithms/exact/field-towers#merge-devices)), and so
    was creating each root of prime power order in a tower of its own
    rather than as a power of a root of a larger order created earlier.
    The trade-off is that knowledge in a long tower is not reused by its
    copies; relations are rediscovered when copies meet, at default
    precision, which is cheap.
12. *Structure beats generality, but not always.* The canonical
    decompositions (prime-power roots of unity, prime radicals) give free
    relations and sparse representations, at the price of longer
    Kronecker arrays. A single cyclotomic generator is faster when it
    fits and prohibitive when many orders occur, so it is an option. A
    primitive element policy for small number fields made products faster
    but caused coefficient growth and slow nested inversions, so it is off
    by default. Several other ideas were tried and dropped after
    measurement: a Trager fallback for square roots in non-quadratic
    towers, univariate gcds for sums of fractions in one transcendental,
    twisted products for towers of small binomial steps, and a subresultant
    gcd for polynomials over quotient rings.
13. *Performance regressions creep in silently.* The `machin` example had
    slipped from 3 s to 68 s at some point: logarithms of large cofactors
    stayed opaque, and every merge of a tower of constant logarithms ran a
    relation search over up to 48 logarithms. Both were fixed (complete
    factorization of word-size cofactors, and skipping searches among
    provably independent logarithms). The eigenvalue identities of the $20
    \times 20$ Hilbert matrix regressed from seconds to minutes when two
    independent changes met in the splitting towers: a recertification of
    absorbed roots of Cauchy moduli at high precision, although the
    enclosures already isolate roots of the origin polynomial, and
    reductions in the deep quotient rings by Newton's preinverse (a power
    series inversion in a nested ring) where ordinary division costs a
    fraction. Skipping the redundant certification, and dividing directly
    for dense moduli of length at most 64, brought $n = 20$ back to about
    10 s. A benchmark harness that compares every case against the
    previous run is the only reliable guard.
14. *Every build configuration finds something.* Runs through FLINT's
    continuous integration (a sanitizer build with assertions, a 32-bit
    build, other architectures) exposed defects that the development
    configuration did not: a method registered with the wrong signature,
    hidden by the cast to a generic function pointer; a literal 62-bit
    prime truncated on 32-bit machines; an adjunction that freed the
    context of its own argument; a leak where generic matrix code moves
    elements with shallow copies; a cache whose cleanup did not reset its
    registration flag; an exported data table declared without
    `FLINT_DLL`; and a test that expected every accurate enclosure of an
    algebraic number to certify.
15. *Writing examples is testing.* Preparing worked examples, each checked
    by a script against the implementation and an independent system,
    found defects that the tests and the benchmark had missed: definitions
    printed in creation rather than dependency order, so that the
    difference of two roots of $x^3 - x - 1$ did not read back; a real
    enclosure printed with a spurious imaginary part; a second generator
    created for $e^1$; and lifetime errors in the Python bindings.
16. *Exactness everywhere, including in the tests.* Cases that selected
    roots by floating-point conversions and compared matrices as strings
    hid differences or produced false alarms. The benchmark now orders
    roots by exact comparisons and selects them by $|r - a| < 1/1000$
    decided in the field, and the tests compare values rather than printed
    forms where the representation is not canonical.

## Testing {#testing}

The test suite combines four kinds of tests.

- *Randomized tests against oracles.* Random algebraic expressions are
  compared with [](qqbar) (`t-qqbar`); random expressions in $\exp$,
  $\log$, $\pi$, roots and trigonometric functions with ball arithmetic,
  plus identities that hold by construction (`t-numeric`); random
  identities of Richardson type (`t-richardson`); random addition
  formulas and their perturbations (`t-trig`); and special function
  identities with perturbed counterparts (`t-special`, `t-hypgeom`,
  `t-modforms`).
- *A catalog of zero-testing problems* (`t-catalog`), parsed from strings
  and run in one shared field in a random order, so that each case meets
  what the others left behind.
- *API coverage tests* (`t-api`, `t-options`, `t-unlimited`,
  `t-factored`, ...) for predicates, coordinates, multiplication
  matrices, maps, conversions, inverse functions on branch cuts against
  [](acb), and options.
- *Stress modes.* The suite is run at ten times the default number of
  iterations, under Valgrind and Helgrind (`t-threads` shares elements
  between threads), in a debug build in which the element union poisons
  its inactive members, and in the build configurations of FLINT's
  continuous integration.

*Example (a randomized identity).* `t-richardson` builds random positive
reals from small rationals, square roots, logarithms of numbers greater
than 1, sums and products, such as $a = \log(1 + \sqrt{5/2})$ and $b =
\sqrt 3\log 3$, and checks one of five identities, here $e^ae^b = e^{a+b}$.
The left side becomes $(1 + \sqrt{10}/2)t_3$ with $t_3 = e^{\sqrt 3\log 3}$,
the right side a new generator $t_4 = e^{\sqrt 3\log 3 + a}$; the zero test
of the difference finds and verifies the relation $\log t_4 = \log t_3 +
a$, after which $t_3$ is expressed as $(\sqrt{10} - 2)t_4/3$, so that the
two sides agree. The perturbed expression $(e^ae^b - e^{a+b} + 3/7)a$
then reduces to $3a/7$, which the test requires to be nonzero.

The profiling script `src/python/gr_tower_profile.py` runs a catalog of
zero-testing and simplification problems on castle arithmetic, Calcium,
`qqbar` and SymPy, each case in a fresh field in a subprocess with a time
limit (or all cases in one shared context, `--shared`), and produces an
HTML report.

## Measurements {#measurements}

The catalog includes the examples and test cases of Calcium and FLINT,
the open issues of Calcium's tracker, SymPy tests and issues, minimal
polynomial and denesting problems (Ramanujan, Shanks and others),
Wester's problems [@Wes1999], trigonometric constants, near misses
(Heegner numbers, ties between sums of square roots at $10^{-20}$, a
hidden zero plus $10^{-20000}$, $e^{10^{-10000}} - 1$), Sage's `QQbar`
doctests and issues, comparisons and signs, branch cuts (including
points $10^{-30}$ from a cut), Richardson-type exponential and logarithm
problems with algebraic arguments, mixed algebraic and transcendental
polynomials, Cardano's and Ferrari's formulas over $\mathbb{Q}$, number
fields and $\mathbb{Q}(\pi, \sqrt 2)$, Machin-like formulas in imaginary
quadratic orders, special function identities, asymptotic inequalities,
matrix functions, number theory benchmarks (Dirichlet's class number
formula as quotients of up to 200 sines, cubic Gauss sums and Gauss's
cubic period polynomials up to $p = 9973$, norms of cyclotomic units,
Mignotte's polynomials), and, since the companion document, hypergeometric
and modular identities. Every case was checked with high-precision
numerics before inclusion.

with a time limit of 20 s per case:

wrong answer is `equals(0)` returning `False` for Ramanujan's identity for

the cases that both decide, Calcium took 0.99 times the time of castle
arithmetic (geometric mean of the ratios), `qqbar` 1.63 times, and SymPy
42 times; per-case times are often dominated by field setup. Castle
arithmetic decided 183 cases that Calcium cannot, 40 that `qqbar` cannot

cannot; no engine decided a case that castle arithmetic could not. The
"time on ok" column is not comparable across rows, since the engines
decide different sets of cases. The catalog has since grown to 871

in total with the default options).

## Open problems {#open}

The following known improvements have not been made; most are standard
techniques.

*Arithmetic and representations.*

- *Dense arithmetic in multi-generator number fields.* Element
  operations in $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt 2, \sqrt 3)$ or in composita of
  cyclotomic fields use the flat `fmpz_mpoly_q` form, with the Kronecker
  product taking about three quarters of a Horner step. A product of two
  elements with coefficients in $\{-9, \ldots, 9\}$ takes about 2.2 µs in
  $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt 2, \sqrt 3, \sqrt 5, \sqrt 7)$ against 0.24–0.30 µs in
  $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{17})$, both of degree 16, and 6–8 µs in
  $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_3, \zeta_5, \zeta_7)$ against 0.9–1.0 µs in
  $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{97})$, both of degree 48: the multi-generator fields
  are about seven times slower than single-generator fields of the same
  degree. A dense tensor representation (with products by per-axis
  twisted wraps for binomial moduli, where Good–Thomas does not apply, or
  by evaluation at split primes) would close most of the gap; it belongs
  in general polynomial functionality of FLINT rather than in the tower
  code. A primitive element kept as a *representation cache* (the value
  also stored in a single-generator field) is a related option that
  avoids changing the canonical form.
- *Fast arithmetic modulo triangular sets.* For towers with non-univariate
  moduli, reduction is generic pseudo-division and inversion goes through
  the multiplication matrix. Techniques for fast arithmetic modulo
  triangular sets [@LMS2009], and fibre-wise modular inversion (for each
  point of the lower levels at a split prime, the roots of the specialized
  modulus, with one Vandermonde transform per fibre), would help at larger
  degrees.
- *Modular inverses.* Fast transforms (Bluestein's algorithm [@Blu1970],
  or subproduct trees) instead of $d \times d$ Vandermonde products for a
  large cyclotomic axis (80% of an inverse in $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{257},
  \sqrt 2)$ is spent on $256 \times 256$ matrices); rational reconstruction
  [@Mon2004] when the adjugate and the norm share large factors (subfield
  elements in large towers); a cost model between modular inversion,
  Dixon's method and the univariate extended gcd; and exact division
  without inversion for fraction-free elimination.
- *Multimodular linear algebra and gcds.* The split-prime machinery makes
  a number field of degree $D$ look like $D$ copies of $\mathbb{F}_p$.
  Determinants and linear systems over number fields, and polynomial gcds
  and resultants over towers, could be computed modulo split primes at
  each point and reconstructed by Chinese remaindering, as in the
  classical modular gcd algorithms over number fields [@LM1989; @Enc1995;
  @vHM2002]. This matters for factoring and root finding as well as for
  linear algebra.
- *Huge coefficients and huge exponents.* About 60% of the time on DFT
  inputs with 80000-bit coefficients is spent copying coefficients;
  monomial products in place and accumulation by swaps should give a
  factor 3–5. Powers $a^n$ of rational functions are expanded (Calcium
  issue #38 at $n = 10^4$ takes seconds); lazy powers, and exact
  recognition of rational ratios among log-side values before LLL (which
  finds $\exp(N\sqrt 2)$ and $\exp((N + 1)\sqrt 2)$ related for any $N$,
  where LLL fails beyond $N \approx 10^4$), are known remedies.
- *Fractions in transcendental generators.* Sums of rational functions in
  $\pi$ (DFT inputs 4 and 5) spend most of their time in multivariate
  gcds. The factored denominators of
  [](algorithms/exact/castle-representations#factored) help in linear
  algebra but not in sums; delayed canonicalization, and evaluation and
  interpolation in the transcendental variables, are the other candidates.
- *Smaller defining polynomials.* Generic algebraic generators keep the
  polynomial they were given. A POLREDBEST-style reduction (LLL on a basis
  of $\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]$, [@Coh1996]) would shrink large defining
  polynomials; neither Calcium nor castle arithmetic does this.
- *Overheads.* A definition-id hash instead of linear scans of the
  registry; caching of the $\Gamma$ lattices per level (the Hurwitz
  lattices are cached); numerical prefilters before the exact zero tests
  in radical and transcendental-generator construction; caching of
  enclosures per element, and evaluation of shared subexpressions once in
  long sums; modular zero tests as a filter before numerics in dense
  towers.

*The zero test.*

- *Tiny and huge arguments.* The Hermite–Lindemann rule handles
  $e^{10^{-10000}} - 1$, but a general treatment of numbers that are tiny
  or huge for structural reasons is missing. A `gr` ring of complex Taylor
  models [@MB2003] (a polynomial in a small parameter $\varepsilon$ with an
  explicit $O(\varepsilon^k)$ error term), tried when ball arithmetic
  fails, would cover cancellations such as $1 - \operatorname{erf}(100)$
  without 14500-bit evaluations; the fully general version needs
  expansions at singularities ($e^x - e^{x'}$ for huge $x$, $x'$). An
  $\operatorname{erfc}$ generator would solve the specific case: since
  $\operatorname{erfc}(x)$ is represented as $1 - \operatorname{erf}(x)$,
  its sign needs $\operatorname{erf}(x)$ to about $x^2/\log 2$ bits
  (129843 bits for $x = 300$, beyond the numerical precision limit),
  whereas Arb evaluates $\operatorname{erfc}(1000) \approx 1.86 \cdot
  10^{-434298}$ directly at 64 bits.
- *The split zero test with more parts.* Sums $f + g + h$ of three or more
  parts with pairwise disjoint generators would need the minimal
  polynomial of a sum of two parts ([](algorithms/exact/zero-testing#split)).
- *Radical policies.* Splitting off rational contents of radicands
  ($\sqrt{34 + 2\sqrt{17}} = \sqrt 2\sqrt{17 + \sqrt{17}}$) adjoins $\sqrt
  2$, which is not in $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{17})$, and doubles the degree in
  Gauss's construction of $\cos(\pi/257)$; keeping the content made that
  case four times faster but another case four times slower. A rule that
  splits only when the root of the content is already present, or becomes
  useful later, is needed. Comparisons of radical expressions with
  cyclotomic numbers could compare minimal polynomials before expressing
  one in the other ($\cos(2\pi/17)$ by Gauss's radicals takes 0.4 s, `qqbar`
  0.01 s).

*Special functions.*

- *Rigorous dilogarithm symbols.* For arguments in a field $F$, a
  combination $\sum c_j\operatorname{Li}_2(x_j)$ is elementary when its
  symbol $\sum_j c_j x_j \wedge (1 - x_j) \in \Lambda^2(F^\times) \otimes
  \mathbb{Q}$ vanishes [@Zag2007]; the rational multiple of $\pi^2$ that
  remains would be recognized numerically, and a proof needs either a
  bound on its denominator or an explicit derivation by five-term
  relations. Only one anharmonic orbit and distribution relations of order
  $n \le 4$ are used today, and orbit points at roots of unity remain a
  gap. For the Rogers dilogarithm $L(x) = \operatorname{Li}_2(x) +
  \frac12\log x\log(1 - x)$, Abel's relation at $x = 1/3$, $y = 1/5$ reads
  $L(\frac13) + L(\frac15) + L(\frac57) + L(\frac{14}{15}) + L(\frac67) =
  \pi^2/2$. Its arguments lie in five different anharmonic orbits; at the
  time of the companion document the zero test of the difference ended as
  `T_UNKNOWN`, and the five-term relation among orbit points of the
  generators' arguments ([](algorithms/exact/castle-special-functions#polylog))
  now decides it; a general procedure for combinations of dilogarithms
  (the symbol above) is still missing.
- *General Chowla–Selberg.* Singular values of $K$ and $E$ beyond $r = 2,
  3, 4$, through $\Gamma$ at rationals of the discriminant's character
  [@ChoSel1967].
- *Isogenies beyond Landen*, keyed by $j$-invariants and detected by
  modular polynomials (see also
  [](algorithms/exact/castle-modular#limitations)).
- *$\Gamma$ relations* at levels beyond 240, and relations mixing $\Gamma$
  on lines with exponential and logarithm generators.
- *Branch cuts.* Elliptic and polylogarithm generators with arguments on
  their cuts are not canonicalized by the reciprocal-modulus and inversion
  formulas; their enclosures may then lose precision, which leads to
  `T_UNKNOWN` (never to a wrong answer).
- *Exponential-algebraic generators* in general (solutions of $x = e^{-x}$
  and similar), of which $W$ is the first instance.

*Features.*

- *Other constant fields*: number fields, finite fields and the $p$-adic
  numbers as the base of towers. The capability bits mark what each
  algorithm needs (a finite field has places and factoring but no complex
  embedding; the $p$-adic numbers have an embedding refined by Hensel's
  lemma but no ordering).
- An embedding object abstracting the complex enclosures (for $p$-adic or
  real-closed embeddings), and folding $\pi$ into the general constant
  kind.
- A shared (DAG) representation of very large moduli, which would be
  needed for splitting towers beyond the Hilbert matrices of size 22.

## Use in FLINT {#use}

- `src/gr_tower/` and `src/gr_ca/` implement castle arithmetic; their
  tests are in `src/gr_tower/test/` and `src/gr_ca/test/`.
- `src/python/gr_tower_profile.py` runs the catalog of zero-testing
  problems against castle arithmetic, Calcium, `qqbar` and SymPy.
- The example programs `dft`, `machin`, `huge_expr`, `hilbert_matrix_ca`
  (with `-castle`) and `number_field_bench` compare castle arithmetic with
  Calcium ([](tutorials/getting-started/example-programs#exact)).
